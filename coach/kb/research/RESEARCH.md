# Research layer (peer-reviewed)

Added 2026-10-16 to close gap K2. YouTube and book notes are mostly expert opinion. This layer records
what controlled studies actually found. Each entry was checked by web search against at least one
primary or institutional source (links below). Figures marked **(press)** came from news coverage
and still need checking against the paper. **Grade**: RCT = randomized trial · MA = meta-analysis ·
OBS = observational (association, not cause).

Habit citations use `source: "res:<key>"`, `locator: "abstract"` (or a table/figure).

| key | Study | Grade | Finding (verified) | Caveats | Informs |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| lally2010 | Lally et al. 2010, "How are habits formed", *Eur J Soc Psychol*, doi:10.1002/ejsp.674 | OBS (n=96, 84 days) | Automaticity rose along a curve and plateaued. **Median ~66 days** to plateau among well-fitted participants, **range 18–254**. | Self-reported automaticity. The 66-day figure comes from the 39 good curve fits. The claim that missing one day didn't matter is disputed between summaries. | all: "this takes ~2 months, not 21 days" |
| gollwitzer2006 | Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006, implementation-intentions meta-analysis, *Adv Exp Soc Psychol* 38:69–119 | MA (94 tests) | If-then plans ("when X, I will Y") had a **medium-to-large effect on goal attainment, d = 0.65**. | d is a standardized difference, not a success rate. Domain-level figures are unverified. | all: when/where field (gap P5) |
| windred2024 | Windred et al. 2024, "Sleep regularity is a stronger predictor of mortality risk than sleep duration", *SLEEP*, PMID 37738616 | OBS (~61k, accelerometer) | The top four regularity quintiles had **~20–48% lower all-cause mortality** than the least regular. Regularity predicted mortality **better than duration**. | Observational. Duration still mattered. | sleep: consistency is scored, not only hours |
| balban2023 | Balban et al. 2023, "Brief structured respiration practices enhance mood and reduce physiological arousal", *Cell Rep Med*, PMC9873947 | RCT (remote, 1 month) | **5 min/day of cyclic sighing improved mood more than 5 min of mindfulness meditation** and lowered respiratory rate (p < 0.05). | One month only. Huberman is a co-author. | deep-breathing: cyclic sighing as the default |
| hunt2018 | Hunt et al. 2018, "No More FOMO", *J Soc Clin Psychol* 37(10):751–768 | RCT (n=143, 3 weeks) | Limiting Facebook, Instagram and Snapchat to **10 min each/day (~30 min)** **reduced loneliness and depression** vs control. The largest gains were in people who started more depressed. | Undergraduates. No between-group change in anxiety or FOMO. | no-social-media: a capped window (not zero) is evidence-backed |
| lindson2016 | Lindson-Hawley et al. 2016, "Gradual versus abrupt smoking cessation", *Ann Intern Med* 164(9):585–592 | RCT (n=697) | **Abrupt quitting beat gradual**: 4-week abstinence **49.0% vs 39.2%**, 6-month **22% vs 15.5%**. Gradual quitters made fewer attempts on quit day. | Unblinded. The authors offer gradual as second-line. | no-nicotine: set a quit day, go abrupt, with support |
| patel2016 | Patel et al. 2016, "Framing financial incentives to increase physical activity", *Ann Intern Med* 164(6):385–394, doi:10.7326/M15-1635 | RCT (n=281, 13 weeks) | A **loss-framed incentive** (start with $42/month, lose $1.40 per missed day) gave a **50% relative increase** in days the 7,000-step goal was met. **An equal gain-framed reward did no better than control.** | No lasting effect after the incentive stopped. | game: "energy you can lose" beats "points you gain" (loss aversion) |
| deci1999 | Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999, extrinsic rewards meta-analysis, *Psychol Bull* 125:627–668 | MA (128 studies) | **Expected, tangible, task-contingent rewards undermined intrinsic motivation** (free-choice d ≈ −0.28 to −0.40). **Positive feedback enhanced it.** | Contested by Cameron & Pierce, though the authors' 2001 follow-up defends it. | game: lead with identity and feedback, not payment; avoid paying for things already enjoyed |
| mazeas2022 | Mazeas et al. 2022, gamification and physical activity meta-analysis of RCTs, *JMIR* 24(1):e26779, PMC8767479 | MA (RCTs 2010–2020) | **Gamified interventions increased physical activity**, but **long-term effects were small and volatile** (novelty fades). | The pooled steps/day figure is unverified. | game: gamification helps, but needs enforcement and meaning to last |
| burns2023 | Burns et al. 2023, daily light exposure and psychiatric disorders, *Nature Mental Health* (UK Biobank, ~86.7k) | OBS | **More daytime light was associated with lower odds of depression, and more night light with higher odds**, after adjusting for shift work and sleep. **(press)**: about −20% (day) and +30% (night) for depression. | Observational. Sample-size figure differs between sources (86,772 vs 86,631). | morning-routine (outdoor light), night-routine (dim lights) |

## What this changes in the plan

1. **The "21 days" myth is wrong.** Tell Conor the real curve: plateau at ~2 months, wide range (lally2010).
2. **When/where is the strongest cheap lever** (gollwitzer2006). Gap P5 moves up to the first build.
3. **Sleep scoring should weight regularity**, not only hours (windred2024). The app already tracks bedtime and wake spread.
4. **Deep breathing defaults to cyclic sighing** (balban2023).
5. **The no-social-media window is evidence-backed**: ~30 min/day, not zero (hunt2018).
6. **Nicotine: abrupt quit day plus support** (lindson2016). Medication and NRT stay with a doctor.
7. **Game design**: loss framing works (patel2016) but can undermine motivation if it feels like payment
   (deci1999), and novelty fades (mazeas2022). Use softened loss (energy) plus identity and feedback plus
   Screen Time enforcement.
