## Claims
- The nervous system grows to the ways in which it has been exercised. [¶16]
- Repetition makes movements simpler, more accurate and less tiring. [¶17]
- Habit reduces the conscious attention an act needs, freeing the mind for other things. [¶25]
- The aim of education is to make the nervous system an ally: automate as many useful actions as early as possible. [¶50]
- Launch a new habit with a strong, decided initiative: gather supports, make incompatible commitments, take a public pledge. [¶51]
- Every day a breakdown is postponed raises the odds it never occurs. [¶51]
- Never allow an exception until the new habit is securely rooted; one lapse undoes many turns, like dropping a ball of string. [¶52]
- Securing success at the outset matters because early failure dampens later effort. [¶54]
- Abrupt change usually beats tapering off, provided you can actually stand it; do not set a task so stiff it guarantees defeat. [¶55]
- A desire dies of inanition if it is never fed. [¶55]
- Making a fresh resolution every day is like stopping at a ditch to take a new run-up; unbroken advance is what accumulates. [¶56]
- Act on each resolution at the first opportunity; good intentions without action leave character unchanged. [¶57]
- Unused emotions get into the habit of evaporating, so act on feelings. [¶60]

## Protocol candidates
- no-nicotine: On quit day remove supplies, tell people, and arrange conditions that make the old behavior hard. [¶51]
- no-nicotine: Treat any "just this once" as breaking the chain; plan the restart in advance. [¶52]
- morning-routine: When a new habit is planned, do it the first time the opportunity arises, not later. [¶57]
- night-routine: Skip fresh daily re-resolving; run the same fixed routine every night. [¶56]
- reset-focus: Start with an easy win so the first week succeeds. [¶54]

## Evidence notes
- An 1890 psychology text; the physiology (nerve "paths") is speculative and dated, and the practical maxims are reasoning drawn from Bain, not experiments.
- Drink and opium examples reflect 19th-century views; the "never an exception" rule is opinion, and modern research tolerates occasional misses.
